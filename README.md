Resolving oxyregulatory capacity at the coral tissue level under declining oxygen conditions

Nicole J. Dilernia1*, David J. Suggett1,2, Michael Kühl3, Mathieu Pernice1, Emma F. Camp1

1Climate Change Cluster, University of Technology Sydney, Ultimo 2007, NSW, Australia

2KAUST Coral Restoration Initiative (KCRI) and Division of Biological and Environmental Science and Engineering (BESE), King Abdullah University of Science and Technology, Thuwal, 23955, Saudi Arabia

3Department of Biology, Marine Biological Section, University of Copenhagen, Strandpromenaden 5, 3000 Helsingør, Denmark

*Corresponding author: Nicole J. Dilernia, Nicole.dilernia@uts.edu.au



Abstract 
Climate-driven ocean warming and deteriorating water quality are escalating ocean deoxygenation and increasing hypoxia risk on coral reefs, where oxyregulatory capacity may become critical for coral persistence under future climates. Regulation of respiration under declining oxygen (O2) can be parameterised by hypoxia response curves (HRC), where respiration rates are expressed as a function of O2 concentrations in the surrounding seawater. However, a dynamic concentration boundary layer (CBL) on the coral tissue surface – influenced by flow, ciliary activity and surface topography – could affect HRC-derived thresholds that may not fully reflect tissue-level O2 conditions. We therefore examined if HRCs derived from closed-system respirometry reflected tissue-level oxyregulation thresholds measured using O2 microsensors across four coral taxa. We revealed greater oxyregulatory capacity in Hydnophora species, with higher total positive regulation (Tpos; 0.52 relative units) and lower maximum regulation capacity (Pcmax; 8.44% air saturation) compared to Montipora species. Additionally, CBL thickness and diffusive O2 fluxes in the dark varied across taxa. Remarkably, we identified a physiological association between oxyregulation and O2 concentrations retained at the tissue surface, suggesting that oxyregulatory capacity reflects the extent to which tissue-O2 deviates from surrounding seawater-O2, highlighting the importance of flow and the CBL when measuring coral hypoxia response.
